Down the Feed

Every app wants to be a rabbit hole. It helps to remember how the first one worked.

Product teams say it without irony: we want users to fall in. It is worth remembering how the original fall began, because Lewis Carroll got the mechanics right a century and a half before anyone wrote a growth strategy.

It began with urgency. The White Rabbit does not invite Alice anywhere. He simply runs past, late, checking his watch, and that is enough. Nobody has ever been persuaded by a rabbit. They have been hurried by one.

The notification is the rabbit's modern descendant. It does not argue that something matters. It implies that something is already happening without you, and that you are, like the rabbit, late. The design work is in the implication, never in the claim.

The label

Then comes a small bottle on a glass table, with a paper label that says “DRINK ME”. It is the most honest onboarding flow in literature: one instruction, no explanation, and a change of scale on the other side.

Alice, to her credit, checks whether it is marked poison first. Most of us skip that step now. The terms are long, the button is bright, and the bottle has already been drunk by everyone we know.

What the label promises is not a destination but a size. You will be small enough to fit through the door, or large enough to see over the garden wall, and either way the room will no longer be the room you were standing in. Software is very good at that kind of promise.

The grin

The interface that stays behind after the product has gone has a patron saint, and it is the Cheshire Cat, who fades away until only the grin is left.

Think of the features that outlive their reasons: the streak that persists after the habit, the badge that remains after the community, the cheerful empty state that greets you in an app nobody maintains. The cat is gone. The grin is still on the screen.

It is tempting to treat that as a bug. It is closer to a business model. A grin is cheap to keep running, and it reassures you that someone, somewhere, is still in a good mood about all this.

The verdict

And at the end of every rabbit hole there is a court. The Queen of Hearts settles every difficulty the same way, by calling for heads, and the joke is that nobody is ever actually executed. The sentence is the spectacle. The verdict is the engagement.

Moderation on large platforms often feels like that court: loud, arbitrary, and somehow both everywhere and nowhere. The pronouncements are constant. The consequences are hard to locate. Everyone is a little afraid and nobody is quite sure of what.

Carroll's way out is the best one available. Alice grows to her full size and says what everyone in the room has been too small to say: they are nothing but a pack of cards. It is not a technical fix. It is a change of scale, which, it turns out, is what the bottle was offering all along.
